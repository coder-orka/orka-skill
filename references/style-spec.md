# Cute cow-themed style specification

## `niulai-cute-3d` (default)

This mode preserves the source subject first and adds a small set of friendly bovine cues. It is a cute cow-themed interpretation, not an official film production guide.

### Reference files

Use the images in `assets/style-reference/` only to study bovine feature placement and rounded mass. Do not copy their stiff expressions, muddy lighting, rough textures, or exact characters into the default mode.

| File | Use for |
|---|---|
| `niulai-calf-closeup.jpg` | horn and ear placement, broad muzzle proportion |
| `niulai-herd-dark.jpg` | varied horn shapes and spacing |
| `niulai-two-cows.jpg` | rounded two-character staging |
| `niulai-wide-trio.jpg` | simple full-body mass and hoof placement |

### Subject preservation

- The output must first read as the same person, pet, animal, or object from the source.
- Preserve subject count, facial structure or species, eye color, coat markings, hairstyle, clothing-color roles, pose, action, left-right order, key props, camera distance, crop, and background layout.
- Add cow features as integrated details. Do not replace the subject with a generic upright cow or unrelated mascot.
- In close portraits, keep face size, gaze direction, cropped edges, and original ear anchor positions stable. Ear anatomy may change only under the conditional cow-ear replacement rule below.

### Cute bovine features

- Use two small rounded horns as the primary cue. Prefer cream, warm ivory, pale caramel, or a source-compatible color. Keep them symmetrical enough to look friendly but not mechanically perfect.
- For a pet or animal, inspect each native ear separately. Treat it as clearly visible only when its base, outer outline, and direction can be read from the source.
- Replace every clearly visible native ear one-for-one with a soft rounded cow ear. Keep the original base position, approximate scale, outward direction, head perspective, and source-compatible color; remove the original ear shape completely.
- Treat the replacement cow ears as the only ears on the entire finished character. When a cow-shaped hood or costume is present, those replacement ears also serve as the hood's ears. Do not add separate ears to the hood's outer sides, crown, or decorative trim.
- Perform a final global anatomy count: the common two-ear source must finish with exactly one cow-ear pair and exactly one horn pair. Horns do not count as ears. Any third or fourth ear is an error, even if it appears attached to clothing rather than anatomy.
- If an ear is hidden by hair, clothing, headwear, crop, pose, or another object, do not invent a replacement cow ear. A one-ear-visible source may correctly produce one cow ear.
- Position horns behind or between the resulting ear anchors, or behind a person's hairline. Never cover the eyes or radically widen the head.
- Inspect the upper chest and under-neck area. When it is clearly visible, add exactly one small bell centered below the neck. Attach it to an existing collar or necklace when possible; otherwise use a narrow soft strap. The bell should hang with gravity, show a credible attachment and contact shadow, and use warm brass/gold or another material treatment coherent with the selected mode.
- When the chest is hidden, covered, or outside the crop, omit the bell. Do not zoom out, reframe, change the pose, or invent anatomy merely to display it.
- Keep the bell small enough that it does not compete with the face or obscure distinctive fur, clothing, or chest markings. Never add multiple, floating, face-level, or oversized bells.
- Add at most one other secondary cue after horns, conditional cow-ear replacement, and the conditional bell: restrained cow spots, a rounded pale muzzle accent, or hoof-like tips when limbs are visible.
- Preserve a pet's species-specific nose and face. A muzzle accent may soften the cheeks but must not turn a cat, dog, or other animal into a cow.
- Use a friendly, curious, calm, or gently surprised expression. Keep eyes expressive and readable without making them enormous or glassy.

### Geometry and materials

- Favor rounded toy-like plush geometry with softly padded volume and an appealing silhouette.
- Keep facial planes smooth and readable. Preserve important source markings and color boundaries.
- For pets and animals, apply the plush conversion to the whole visible subject, including the head, cheeks, muzzle surround, neck, torso, chest, tail, and visible limbs. The hood and the animal body must belong to one coherent stuffed-plush world.
- Use a short, dense, even plush pile with soft fill underneath across the animal's full visible silhouette. Preserve coat patterns and color boundaries as changes in plush color and pile direction. Keep the surface velvety and compact rather than stringy, looped, or shaggy.
- Retain crisp, recognizable eyes, nose, mouth, whiskers, claws, and other identity-critical details as appropriate inset or attached toy components. Plush stylization must not erase the animal's species or expression.
- Add only a small number of clean doll seams where a real stuffed animal, hood, or accessory would be assembled, such as along body contours or joint transitions. Keep seams subtle, structurally plausible, and away from the eyes, nose, mouth, and identity-defining markings.
- Explicitly exclude loose yarn, knitted stitches, rib-knit patterns, crochet holes, woven loops, braided fibers, and visible wool strands. Do not interpret “plush” as yarn craft.
- Use soft handcrafted texture, subtle fur or fabric cues, and mild surface variation. Avoid both photoreal grafting and intentionally broken low-resolution textures.
- Horns should feel attached to the same character, with compatible lighting and material response.

### Scene, light, and color

- Preserve the source environment and spatial relationships unless the user asks for a replacement.
- Rebuild edited scenes consistently enough that horns and secondary features do not look pasted onto a photograph.
- Use warm diffuse light, gentle contact shadows, moderate saturation, and clear subject/background separation.
- Retain the source palette, then add small cream, caramel, blush, or pastel accents for bovine features.

### What misses the style

- Replacing the source subject with a generic cow, mascot, or bovine-headed human.
- Losing facial identity, species, coat pattern, clothing, pose, action, crop, or key props.
- For cat portraits, changing the cat face, source eye color such as blue-green, cow hood, pose, or original sofa/background scene.
- Giant sharp horns, realistic taxidermy horns, demonic silhouettes, extra limbs, or accessories covering the face.
- Keeping clearly visible native ears while adding a second pair of cow ears, inventing cow ears where source ears are hidden or cropped, or adding separate side/decorative ears to a cow hood.
- Omitting the bell when the upper chest is clearly visible, or adding a bell when its placement area is outside the crop.
- A floating, duplicated, face-level, or oversized bell; a bell that hides distinctive markings; or a crop changed only to make room for it.
- A pale rubber muzzle that erases the original nose and mouth.
- Creepy slit eyes, stiff hostile expressions, dirty stretched textures, muddy lighting, or deliberate amateur ugliness in the default mode.
- Yarn, knit, crochet, woven-loop, braided-fiber, or excessively seamed surfaces instead of short dense plush.
- A photographic or ordinary-fur animal wearing a plush hood; in the default mode, the animal subject itself must also read as short-dense plush with soft filling.
- Generic cute 3D with no clearly visible bovine cue.
- Copying a named studio look; describe the result through concrete shape, material, color, and lighting traits.

## `niulai-film-rough` (explicit only)

When the user explicitly requests the rough film-frame look, use balloon torsos, mask-like heads, pale rubber muzzles, small half-lidded eyes, uneven blunt horns, pasted low-resolution textures, primitive stock environments, flat light, weak shadows, and mild screen-capture softness. Keep subject invariants unless the user explicitly permits full character replacement.

## `niulai-poster-ink` (explicit only)

Use white paper, large negative space, black and gray mountain ink washes, deep green water strokes, a tiny red-cloaked bovine figure, vertical calligraphic composition, and restrained mineral pigments. Do not mix this with cute 3D or rough film-frame references.
